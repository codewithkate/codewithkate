## Experimental Design

**Platform:** Online survey experiment via Prolific or similar panel, targeting N ≈ 2,400 partisans (Democrats and Republicans, excluding pure independents).

**Structure:** A modified dictator game with a 2 (scarcity vs. abundance) × 3 (co-partisan, out-partisan, no party information control) between-subjects design, with participant party as a blocking variable.

**Scarcity manipulation:** Before the allocation task, participants receive a brief, factual framing about the economic context of the game:

- **Abundance condition:** "You have been given $10. There are plenty of bonus funds remaining in our research budget, so additional participants will also receive endowments."
- **Scarcity condition:** "You have been given $10. Research funds are limited, and not all participants will receive an endowment. You are one of the few selected."

This manipulation is lightweight and does not change the actual decision parameters (everyone allocates from $10), but it shifts the perceived context from abundance to zero-sum competition—a manipulation validated in behavioral economics research on scarcity mindsets.

**Partisan information treatment:** Participants see a capsule profile of Player 2 (as in Iyengar & Westwood's Study 3), including age, gender, income, and—critically—either Democratic, Republican, or no party identification.

**Primary dependent variable:** Amount allocated to Player 2 (0–10 scale).

**Key decomposition:** By comparing allocations to co-partisans, out-partisans, and the no-information control within each scarcity condition, we can separately estimate:

- The **in-group bonus** (co-partisan allocation minus control allocation)
- The **out-group penalty** (control allocation minus out-partisan allocation)
- Whether scarcity differentially magnifies one versus the other
