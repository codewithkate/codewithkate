**Study 1: Survey Experiment (Conjoint)**

Recruit ~2,000 respondents via a platform like Lucid or YouGov. Respondents evaluate pairs of professional profiles (a paired conjoint design) for hypothetical professionals across six domains, three stereotypically "Republican" (financial planning, military strategy consulting, energy engineering) and three stereotypically "Democratic" (public health advising, social work, environmental science consulting). Each profile randomly varies:

- **Party identification** (Democrat / Republican / not stated)
- **Credential quality** (top-tier vs. mid-tier credential)
- **Years of experience** (5 vs. 15 years)
- **Gender** (male / female)

Outcome measures:

1. Perceived competence in their professional role (7-point scale)
2. Willingness to hire this person for a relevant task (binary + confidence scale)
3. Expected quality of advice from this person (7-point scale)

The key test is the **three-way interaction**: Party of professional × Domain stereotype alignment × Party of respondent. If prototype-based competence attribution exists, we should see:

- **In-group favoritism** (replicating Iyengar & Westwood): people generally prefer co-partisan professionals.
- **Stereotype-congruent competence bonus**: a Republican financial advisor gets a competence boost _even from Democratic respondents_, and a Democratic public health advisor gets a boost even from Republican respondents—but only in the stereotype-matched domain.
- **Credential sensitivity moderation**: following Carlin & Love's finding that real information overrides partisan stereotypes in reciprocity decisions, strong credentials should attenuate the partisan competence bias, while weak or ambiguous credentials should amplify it.

**Study 2: Incentivized Behavioral Measure**

To move beyond hypothetical ratings (addressing concerns from Easton & Holbein about the gap between survey responses and real behavior), conduct an incentivized advice-taking task. Participants receive a $5 endowment and must decide how much to follow the advice of a randomly assigned "advisor" on an estimation task (e.g., estimating county-level statistics). The advisor's partisan identity and domain are randomized. Participants earn a bonus based on accuracy. The dependent variable is weight-of-advice: how much participants adjust their initial estimate toward the advisor's recommendation. This provides a behavioral, incentive-compatible measure of how much competence people impute from partisan cues in professional contexts.

**Study 3: Correction Experiment**

Drawing on Ahler & Sood's finding that informing people about actual party composition reduces perceived out-party extremity, provide a random subset of participants with accurate information about the actual professional composition of each party (e.g., "X% of financial professionals identify as Democrats; Y% identify as Republicans"). Test whether this correction attenuates the stereotype-congruent competence bias identified in Studies 1 and 2.
